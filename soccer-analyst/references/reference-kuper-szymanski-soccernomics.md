# Soccernomics (2022 World Cup Edition) — Simon Kuper & Stefan Szymanski
**Format**: pdf | **Pages**: ~518 | **Sections**: 21 chapters in 3 parts (Clubs / Fans / Countries) | **Depth**: study
**Use for**: transfer decisions, manager and sacking calls, myth-busting, national-team expectations, and "is this club actually badly run?"

## Mental Model (read first)
Two authors, two methods, deliberately combined: Szymanski is "trained to torture the data until they confess"; Kuper "goes around interviewing people." Their shared premise is that **much in soccer can be explained, even predicted, by studying data — especially data found outside soccer.**

The book's governing claim is that football "had escaped the Enlightenment." Clubs are run "mostly by men who do what they do because they have always done it that way," and the game is "full of unexamined clichés" — "Soccer is big business," "hosting a World Cup is an economic bonanza," "the Premier League is holding back England" — none of which had been tested against data.

Three load-bearing conclusions run through everything:
1. **Wages predict results; transfers mostly waste money.** Salaries explain roughly **90% of the variation in league position over the long run**. The transfer market, by contrast, is riddled with systematic biases — which makes it the place where a smart club can most easily gain.
2. **Clubs are not businesses.** They are trophy-maximisers, not profit-maximisers, and they almost never die. That single fact explains most of what looks like irrationality.
3. **Most managers don't matter much** — because the player market is efficient and transparent, while the manager market is neither.

And the intellectual honesty rule they state explicitly and then demonstrate: in 2009 they predicted the rest of the world would catch Western Europe. It didn't. "We're with the economist John Maynard Keynes: **when the facts change, we change our minds.**"

**The opening parable, worth carrying**: Manchester City's analysts studied 400+ corners and found the **inswinger to the near post** most dangerous (with the odd outswinger mixed in — "a mixed strategy"). Mancini, an ex-player, rejected it: outswingers produce *beautiful* goals, and beautiful goals stick in the memory. Later, via assistant David Platt, City switched anyway — and scored 15 goals from corners in 2011–12, more than anyone, ten of them from inswingers, including Kompany's title-sealing header. **Memory selects for beauty; data doesn't.**

## Frameworks & Structure

### PART I — The Clubs

#### Ch 2 — Gentlemen Prefer Blonds: how to avoid silly mistakes in the transfer market
The core chapter for any transfer question. Two halves: the **named inefficiencies**, then the **masters** who exploited them.

**Systematic inefficiencies (not one-off errors — "deviations from rationality"):**
- **A new manager wastes money.** He buys his own players and clears out his predecessor's at a discount — "on the pretense that he is reshaping the club for many years to come, even though in practice he almost always leaves pretty rapidly." Case: Di Canio at Sunderland — six months, thirteen games, ~$30m spent, fourteen in, fifteen out, and the club left bottom for his successor. The structural cause, per Billy Beane: "**The person who has access to the greatest expenditure in the business has no risk in the decision making.**" No manager gets a bonus for a profit. (Beane's exception: "When I think of Arsène Wenger, I think of Warren Buffett. Wenger runs his football club like he is going to own the club for one hundred years.")
- **Stars of recent World Cups or Euros are overvalued.** The worst time to buy is the summer after a big tournament: everyone has seen him, he's exhausted, and quite possibly sated. Ferguson, on Jordi Cruyff and Poborský after Euro 96: "I didn't receive the kind of value their countries did that summer." And the sample is tiny — Arsenal's John Jensen scored one long-range goal in a Euro final and **one goal in four years at Arsenal**. This is the **availability heuristic**: the more available a piece of information, the more it influences the decision, even when irrelevant.
- **Big names as marketing, not football.** Real Madrid's shooting stars are "best understood as marketing gifts to a club's fans, its sponsors, and the local media." Ferguson on the Ronaldo fee: "It was a way for Florentino Pérez to say to the world, 'We are Real Madrid, we are the biggest of the lot.'" Raiola on Real: "**Another player for the cabinet. A trophy player, I call it.**" And Biermann's Bundesliga president on his coach: "My car, my house, my star signing!" — conspicuous consumption, where the very pointlessness proves you can afford to waste.
- **Certain nationalities are overvalued.** Keller: "in the transfer market, it's good to be Dutch." An agent to Alex Bellos: "it is much easier selling a crap Brazilian than a brilliant Mexican." Post-2014 the Costa Rican transfer market went from $922,000 to ~$10m in a year. **A wise club buys unfashionable nationalities at a discount.**
- **Gentlemen prefer blonds.** One big English club noticed its youth scouts kept recommending blonds — because on a field of twenty-two unknowns, the colour catches the eye. Same family as Beane's "sight-based prejudices" in baseball. (Cox's counterpoint on the other side: Solskjær abandoned a scouting trip because the player had a Mohawk.)
- **Budget allocation across the XI.** Szymanski and Guy Wilkinson found the decisive player is neither the best nor the worst but **the second-best**, and that the optimal split of the transfer budget is roughly **26/26/18/10/10/10 and ~0 for the bottom five**. Clubs in practice spread money far more evenly — plausibly for dressing-room, injury-cover or availability reasons, but the authors think "an innovative club could do well by concentrating its budget upward." Chris Anderson's refinement: rather than chase scarce superstars, assemble **productive combinations** — "Who plays well with whom?"

**The masters, and what they actually did:** Clough and Taylor at Forest (European champions while turning a *profit* on transfers — Birtles bought for $3,500 and sold for $2.9m; Keane for $80,000, sold for $5.6m; Kenny Burns bought as "a fighting, hard-drinking gambler… a stone overweight" and voted player of the year the next season); Wenger's first decade; and Lyon's seven straight French titles — which ended precisely when they started paying big fees for "stars" (Gourcuff).

**The summarising rule**: "Soccer clubs need to make fewer transfers… **Any inefficient market is an opportunity for somebody.** If most clubs are wasting most of their transfer money, then a club that spends wisely is going to outperform." Note also the quiet finding on economy: **Tottenham** kept a Big Six place while spending far below its rivals.

#### Ch 3 — The Worst Business in the World
- **Soccer is not big business.** Barcelona's pre-COVID record revenue of ~€841m was "just 4 percent of United Natural Foods' revenues" — a food distributor most people have never heard of — and would have made Barça only the 52nd-largest company in Finland. UEFA's HQ "looks like the offices of a small insurance company." **A single large supermarket is a bigger business than all but a handful of clubs.**
- **Why: appropriability.** Clubs "have not been able to make money out of more than a tiny share of our love of soccer." Most of the game is consumed as talk, screens, forums and video games. "Chelsea cannot charge us for talking or reading or thinking about Chelsea." De Zeeuw: "the whole world earns money from your success as a player." **The world earns more from soccer than the soccer industry does.**
- **Soccer is also a *bad* business, and every good idea came from outside it.** Clubs once *paid* Umbro and Adidas to supply their kit; England hoped for a discount in 1966 and got the kit free. It was Murdoch who went to the clubs about satellite TV, not the reverse; the clubs banned live league TV until 1982 for fear of losing gate revenue (the research says the loss is tiny). Stadium renovation only happened because the **Taylor Report forced it** — and then crowds grew. Hence the chant correction: "**Where were you when your stadium was shit?**"
- **The two paths, and both fail.** Sugar ran Spurs within its means for a decade: one League Cup, mid-table, ~$3m a year profit, crowds *down* 5% while the league rose 29%. **"When businesspeople try to run a soccer club as a business, not only does the soccer suffer, but so does the business."** The other path — win first, profits follow — also fails: plotting profit against league position for Premier League clubs 2011–2020, half the dots are losses and there is barely any relationship. In 45% of cases position and profit moved in *opposite* directions (chance alone would give 50%).
- **The proof that clubs are win-maximisers, not profit-maximisers**: Szymanski and Pedro Garcia del Barrio estimated that a profit-maximising **Barcelona would aim to finish 15th** and a profit-maximising **Real Madrid 17th**, with Atlético, Athletic, Sevilla and Villarreal best off in the second division. In reality Spanish clubs finished on average **twelve places above their profit-maximising position and less than half a place below their win-maximising one.**
- **The arms race that makes it rational**: as long as some owners will spend whatever it takes, everyone must. Spanish first-division revenues rose fourteenfold in a decade and the wage share barely moved (~62%; 93% in the second division). A. T. Kearney's line, endorsed: **clubs are "nothing more than vessels for transporting soccer's income to players."**

#### Ch 4 — Safer Than the Bank of England
- **Core idea**: "The notion that soccer clubs are inherently unstable businesses is wrong." Lehman Brothers was 148× Manchester United's revenue and no longer exists; almost every European club of 2008 does. Of the 88 English league clubs of 1923, **85 still existed in 2021–22 and 70 were still in the top four divisions**; 38 were in the same division.
- **Why they survive**: rivals don't celebrate a rival's death ("clubs know they cannot operate without opponents" — Arsenal wrote Orient a cheque in 1931), and above all **"phoenixing"**: the club is a club *and* a limited company, so the company can be dissolved and reborn debt-free while the club plays on.
- **The consequence, which is the real payoff**: because clubs practically always survive, **"they can afford to tolerate stupidity."** Punishment for incompetence is limited to relegation. This is the deep explanation for almost every irrationality in the book.

#### Ch 5 — A Decent Business at Last? (Be Careful What You Wish For)
- **Amortization — the single most useful accounting concept for reading football finance.** Since the 1990s a transfer fee is written off across the length of the contract: $30m over three years is $10m a year in the accounts. So **"when a club reports losses because of heavy amortization, it's quite possible that there was no actual money going out."** In 2018–19 Premier League clubs reported £424m operating losses *and* £1.3bn of amortization charges — "none of which is 'real' money actually paid out. Add this back, and the pretax loss turns into a healthy 'cash' surplus."
- **The club as a cashbox.** United reported a £20m pretax loss in 2020–21 with EBITDA of £96m. Big clubs "are becoming like banks" — cash-rich without being profitable — which is why the Glazers could buy United with United's own cash flow, and why private equity (RedBird, Elliott, Silver Lake, CVC) has arrived.
- **FFP failed on its own terms.** In 2009, before the rules, clubs spent 63.9% of revenue on wages; in 2019, **64.0%**. Clubs spending over 80% of revenue on wages rose from 24.3% to 29.3%. The authors' verdict: the rules "were designed to limit competition, not to keep clubs solvent," they are largely unenforceable under European law, and their real beneficiaries are the incumbent "aristocrats."
- **The authors' counterintuitive preference**: "**Hurrah for the sugar daddies.**" Their model of what a club run *as a business* looks like is Arsenal under Kroenke: "high ticket prices, little desire to win trophies, and big profits" — valuation up from ~$1.17bn to $2.8bn over a decade of disappointment. If cost controls cut wages, "more likely, they would go into the offshore accounts of billionaire owners."

#### Ch 6 — Super League: why rich people don't always get what they want
- **The economic logic was real**: in the decade to 2021, the twelve founders based in different countries played each other **ninety times in total**; a home-and-away Super League would produce ninety such matches *every season*. The draw was never club rivalry but **player match-ups** — "Messi against Kane, Neymar against Salah."
- **What killed it**: "The fans protested for the right to lose." Supporters saw themselves as "members of a broader community," treated the owner as a **steward, not an owner**, and hated the loss of competition most of all. "End this system, and you kill hope."
- **The comparative evidence they use**: American baseball had 47 minor leagues in 1912; TV killed them because minor-league teams had **no hope of promotion**. Europe keeps 700+ top-tier professional clubs for roughly twice the US population. Promotion and relegation is the difference.
- **Why the plotters blundered**: soccer's tolerance of stupidity again. Five of the twelve clubs were run by heirs. "Isolated in their mansions… they never seem to have thought of getting fans, politicians, and media onside."

#### Ch 7 — Unbanned: the case for reparations for women's soccer
- **The suppressed history, which is the argument**: Dick, Kerr Ladies drew **53,000 at Goodison Park** on Boxing Day 1920 — more than almost any men's professional club at the time. In December 1921 the FA banned women from members' grounds ("quite unsuitable for females"). Canada followed in 1922, Norway 1931, Germany 1955, Denmark 1963. The bans lasted about half a century.
- **Title IX (1972) is the counterfactual made real.** US female high-school sports participation went from ~7% in 1971 to over 40% today, and no school sport grew faster than soccer. Result: the US men have never come close to a World Cup since 1930; **the US women have won four of eight World Cups and four Olympic golds.**
- **The investment asymmetry, stated as a question worth reusing**: WUSA folded after $100m; MLS lost ~$350m over the same span and its investors stayed. "If someone offered you the chance to take one of two products to market, would you choose the one for which there were dozens of producers better than you, or the one for which you were the best producer on Earth?"
- **The authors implicate themselves**, which is why the chapter carries weight: "The first edition of *Soccernomics*, published in 2009, barely mentioned women's soccer… We are part of the problem."

#### Ch 8 — Need Not Apply: does soccer discriminate against Black people?
- **The historical mechanism**: clubs "used to 'know' that Black players 'lacked bottle,' and they therefore overpaid mediocre white players." Wage discrimination on the field died because **the market in players is transparent** — an underpaid player gets bid away.
- **The general law to carry forward**: "**Efficient markets punish discrimination in plain view of everyone… Inefficient markets can maintain discrimination almost indefinitely.**"

#### Ch 9 — Do Coaches Matter? The Cult of the White Manager
- **The headline finding**: of ~700 English managers observed over 30+ games between 1973 and 2010, **at most about 10% look like overachievers** once wage spending is controlled for. Re-run for 2010–11 to 2020–21 on 10,206 games and 181 managers using Transfermarkt squad valuations: **only twenty had a statistically significant positive effect — just over 10%.** (That list includes Guardiola, Ferguson, Klopp, Conte, Mancini, Pochettino, Rodgers, Wenger, Mourinho, plus Bielsa at Leeds, Chris Wilder at Sheffield United, Valérien Ismaël at Barnsley, and both of Brentford's managers.) The 1973–2010 top four: **Paisley, Ferguson, Bobby Robson, Wenger.**
- **Why managers matter less than assumed**: wages explain ~90% of long-run league position, so there is little residual left. Cruyff: "If your players are better than your opponents, 90 percent of the time you will win." Tuchel: soccer is "a players' game," not "a coaches' game." Tenure collapsed from 3.5 years in 1992 to **1.29 years** for the 58 managers sacked in 2015–16; the median across Germany, France, Spain and Italy 2001–2015 was **350 days**. And the job has been distributed — Wenger: "The manager isn't a lonely man anymore."
- **The mediocrity trap (Terviö's theory, tested by Szymanski and Peeters)**: because ability is only revealed on the job and revealed stars then sell themselves to the highest bidder, firms **underinvest in finding talent and overinvest in known mediocrities**. **A full third of managers were mediocre** in the precise sense that their estimated ability was below that of the average new entrant. Ranieri is the case study: 29 years without an outstanding success, "the perfect loser, with a capital L," then a title, then a *Harvard Business Review* article, then sacked after five wins in twenty-five.
- **The sacking bounce is regression to the mean.** Bridgewater: clubs typically sack when averaging **1.0 point per game** against a norm of **1.3**; three months after a sacking the club is back at 1.3. "The new manager rarely causes the pendulum to swing. He's just the beneficiary of the swing."
- **What managers are actually for**: figurehead and PR. "The manager is more of a king, or a head of public relations, than an executive." Macri on Bilardo at Boca: "fortunately we could put most of the damage on Bilardo… Coaches function as **fuses** — when you lose three matches in a row, you fire them." Barney Ronay: "The manager was born to be sacked."
- **Where a manager genuinely can add value**: (a) **national teams**, where he can import knowledge a country lacks (Capello for England, Hiddink for South Korea, Australia, Russia); (b) **restraining transfer spending** — "Wenger was a better manager than Benitez in part because he blew less of his budget on transfers."
- **Wenger's rise and decline, explained as an arbitrage that closed**: he arrived with knowledge nobody in England had (diet, supplements, statistics, and above all foreign scouting — "hardly any other manager in England in the mid-1990s scouted abroad"). Then everyone copied him. "**Knowledge gaps inside a league close fast.**"

#### Ch 10 — Isaac Newton, Liverpool, and the Moneyball of Soccer
- **Core idea, and the chapter's title metaphor**: "data analytics in sport is now in the **alchemy phase** of development" — a frantic, secretive quest with limited theory, conducted for profit. Alchemy did found modern chemistry; that's the optimistic half.
- **The verifiable test they apply to Liverpool** — and it's a template: over 2011–2020 Liverpool spent **1.7× the Premier League average** for an average finishing position of **4.8**; Chelsea and United averaged one position higher on **2× average**; City won four titles on **2.2×**. Verdict: "**This isn't spectacular overperformance.**" (Their tongue-in-cheek nomination for a real Moneyball: **Tottenham**, comparable position on barely above average spending.)
- **A caution about attribution**: "even over the course of a decade, just one or two transfers can make a big difference" — Coutinho bought for £8.5m and sold for up to £142m. Well timed both ways, but not a system.
- **The dog that didn't bark**: nobody inside Barcelona ever claimed tiki-taka came from analytics. "World-beating innovation didn't seem to require analytics."
- **Brentford and Midtjylland (Benham and Ankersen) — three explicit methods, the most actionable list in the book:**
  1. **Take the road less traveled.** Transfermarkt valuations forecast results very well in big leagues and poorly in thin ones (e.g. League Two) — so hunt where the market is thin. Midtjylland's mantra: **"Distrust your eyes."** Ankersen: "If you base your opinion of a player on a few games you've attended, it will blur your vision. It's a small sample."
  2. **Perfect techniques that others neglect — above all set pieces.** About **one-third of goals come from set-pieces**, which are static and therefore analysable and *controllable*. Yet in 2010–11 the average Premier League team scored on just **one in thirty-five direct free kicks**, because taking them is "a superstar's perk." A free kick is really a passing opportunity: opponents must retreat ten yards and commit two or three to a wall. Midtjylland at one point scored nearly a goal a game from free kicks; Brentford believes set pieces still give it a **0.5 goal per game advantage**, and four of its five set-piece coaches have been poached by Premier League clubs.
  3. **Listen to the wisdom of the diversified crowd.** Brentford uses fan forums — with the caveat that "if everyone in the crowd believes exactly the same thing, then what you have is the opinion of a cult, not a crowd."
  Plus a fourth, quieter one: Brentford "is looking for ways to **downgrade the manager's role in decision making** — an inefficiency that hampers almost all other clubs."
- **Open source beats closed shop**, is the authors' bet: "a crowd of experts testing ideas will generally end up knowing more than a small, secretive group."

#### Ch 11 — The Economist's Fear of the Penalty Kick
- **Core idea**: the penalty is the purest real-world instance of **game theory** — a zero-sum, non-cooperative game where the right choice depends entirely on the opponent's choice. Soriano's Gato Díaz states the regress perfectly: "Constante kicks to the right." … "But he knows that I know." … "Yeah, but I know that he knows." … "No. He knows that I know that he knows."
- **The Moscow 2008 story, which is the chapter's proof**: a Basque economist told Chelsea that **van der Sar tended to dive right against right-footed kickers**. Terry aimed left; van der Sar duly went right; Terry slipped and missed by inches. "By one estimate, Terry's penalty cost Chelsea $170 million" — and Avram Grant was sacked, "even though he is exactly the same manager as if the ball had gone in."
- **The transferable point about mixed strategies**: the equilibrium requires randomising, not picking a favourite corner. Same logic as the corner-kick opening (mostly inswingers, some outswingers "just to keep the opponents guessing").

#### Ch 12 — The Suburban Newsagents: city sizes and soccer prizes
- **Core idea**: European Cup history splits into three eras defined by **city type**, not club quality.
  1. **Totalitarian capitals (1956–late 1960s).** Eight of the first eleven European Cups went to Real Madrid or Benfica; seven of the first sixteen losing finalists also came from fascist capitals. Later: Steaua 1986, Red Star 1991. Mechanism: "Dictators send resources to the capital." Franco's foreign minister called Real "**the best embassy we have ever had**."
  2. **The curse of the democratic capital.** For the trophy's first forty-two years, no democratic capital won it (Amsterdam being nominal only — the government sits in The Hague). Nottingham alone has more than London, Paris, Rome, Berlin, Istanbul and Moscow had combined before 2012. Marseille won it; PSG hadn't. Milan and Turin have twelve; Rome zero.
  3. **The provincial industrial town**, "where a soccer club can mean everything." The explanation is psychological: "**Capitals simply have less to prove than provincial cities. They have bigger sources of pride than their soccer teams.**"

### PART II — The Fans
Loyalty, suicides and happiness. The reusable findings: **hosting a World Cup doesn't make you rich but does make you happier** (the effect is on well-being, not GDP); the **Nick Hornby model of monogamous lifelong fandom is a minority case** — most fans are better described as serial, polygamous or "clients"; and English football's suicide-rate research suggests the game can bind people rather than break them. Use this part to check any claim that a tournament will pay for itself, or that "real fans" behave a certain way.

### PART III — Countries

#### Ch 17 — The Curse of Poverty
Poor countries are poor at *sports*, not just at soccer: nutrition, coaching, facilities and the sheer cost of specialising all compound. The chapter's fieldwork (Johannesburg, the Cape Flats) makes the point that talent is not the constraint — **the conversion of talent into trained athletes is.**

#### Ch 18 — Core Beats Periphery: why little Western Europe rules
- **The corrected prediction, stated plainly**: the first edition's subtitle promised the US, Japan, Australia, Turkey and Iraq would become kings. "Well, we were wrong." Since 2006 Western Europe has won **four straight World Cups**, two of them outside Europe, and continental Europeans took **eleven of the twelve podium places** from 2006 to 2018, across **seven different countries**. "There is always a neighbor coming through."
- **The explanation is networks, not talent.** Western Europe is mild, rainy, fertile, densely populated and — per Ruthven — has "a higher ratio of coast to landmass than any other continent." From the 2006 World Cup you could fly to ~20 countries and 300 million people in two and a half hours. From Tokyo in 2002: Seoul. From São Paulo: Asunción. "**That is the densest network on Earth.**"
- **The one non-European exception is a network too**: Montevideo and Buenos Aires, "separated only by an easy boat ride," played the first international outside the British Isles and North America in 1901.
- **Britain's fall fits the same model**: it was the centre of the knowledge network until roughly WWI — which is why a head coach is still "**il mister**" in Italy and Spain — and then the centre moved across the Channel.

#### Ch 19 — Why England Still Loses — But Nowadays Only Just
- **The false explanation, killed with its own statistic.** "Too many foreigners" is import-substitution reasoning, and it protects bad producers. Englishmen played "only" 37% of Premier League minutes — which is **over seventy Englishmen per match day, more than any other nationality in one of the world's best leagues**, plenty for a squad of twenty-two, and far more top-level experience than Croats or Uruguayans get. And the record improved after Bosman: four quarter-finals in thirteen attempts in the "British league" era (1968–92) versus **five in eleven** in the international era (1998–2018).
- **The model: what should England expect?** Regressing goal difference on **population, income per head and experience**, plus home advantage, across the Jürisoo database of international results. The coefficients are the part to remember:
  - **Home advantage ≈ a full goal per game** in 2000–2019 (up from about two-thirds of a goal in 1980–2001) — the largest single factor.
  - **Doubling your international experience ≈ +0.45 goals per game.**
  - **Doubling income per head ≈ +0.08 goals per game** (one goal every twelve games).
  - **Doubling population ≈ one goal every thirty-three games** — almost nothing.
  > "Being large and rich helps a country win matches, but **having a long soccer history helps a lot more**. This is why the Portuguese and Croats win more than huge but inexperienced China and India."
- **The verdict**: England is "an experienced, rich, but **medium-sized** competitor" — 56 million against Germany's 83m and France's 67m — and ranks about 8th–10th, which is roughly what the model expects. Its win percentage is ~69%. **A 69% chance per game compounds to barely 10% over the six or seven wins a World Cup requires** — and less against elite opposition. "England simply should not expect to be world-beaters."
- **The cheap-battery problem** — a genuinely diagnostic finding. In every World Cup ever, most goals come in second halves; England is the exception. In its eleven big tournaments 1998–2021 it scored 41 of 72 goals **before** half-time, and in the matches in which it was *eliminated* between 1992 and 2021, **twelve of fourteen goals came before half-time, six of those twelve in the opening five minutes.**
  And crucially, the popular explanation is wrong: Szymanski and Wilkinson found **no link between games played and results** across 10,000+ Premier League matches. "Scheduling is not the problem it is often made out to be… these excuses rarely appear if the team wins." The real cause is in-match: **high tempo, low possession, in summer heat, against teams that don't give the ball back.** "It's that they exhaust themselves in the course of each game."
- **Luck, taken seriously.** "The difference between an English team being considered legendary or a failure is two to three games, each generally decided by a single goal, over two years." Wilson, quoted approvingly: "One moment can shape a game, and one game can shape a tournament, and one tournament can shape a career… **there is so little of it huge conclusions are drawn from individual games.**" Wenger's version: a European Championship lasts about three weeks, and any team in a league can be top after three weeks.
- **The Marcos story** is the counterexample that proves how rare true dominance is: Brazil won 2002 with a goalkeeper whose wrist had never healed, who could barely catch a ball and never dared tell Scolari.

#### Ch 20 — Tom Thumb: the best little soccer country on Earth
- **The question**: not who is best absolutely (Brazil, still) but **who is best relative to population, income and experience** — and who most underachieves.
- **Method note worth stealing**: World Cups are too small a sample ("the difference between Argentina's two and England's one comes down to as little as Maradona's 'hand of God'"), so use all ~940 internationals a year, and use **goal difference per game** rather than win percentage, because "any two wins are not the same."
- **Top ten by win percentage 2000–2021**: Spain (.786), Brazil (.756), France (.731), **Iran (.716)**, Netherlands (.713), England (.712), Argentina (.708), Germany (.703), Portugal (.702), Italy (.688).
- **The Brazil finding, which is a caution against era-narratives**: Brazil's win percentage sat between **73% and 78% in every decade from the 1950s onward**, including the supposedly fallow post-1970 years — while in the 1910s–20s it was ~50%. "Their hubris at [1950] had no historical basis." **The broader story is consistent excellence; the tournament narratives are noise.**

#### Ch 21 — The Future
Despite COVID, "the game has never had it so good." The growth is now global and streamed; the risks are ownership extraction and the erosion of the pyramid, not demand.

## Worked Example
**Why the Moscow 2008 shoot-out is the whole book in one incident.**

Chelsea had done the analytics: a Basque economist had established that **van der Sar dived right against right-footed kickers**. That is exactly the kind of edge the book argues is available — a small, cheap, specific, empirically-grounded advantage in a static, controllable situation, of the same family as Brentford's set pieces or City's inswinging corners.

Terry aimed left. Van der Sar dived right, as predicted. And Terry slipped on wet grass and missed by inches.

Then everything downstream of that inch: Chelsea lost the Champions League (estimated cost, $170m) and **Avram Grant was sacked — "even though he is exactly the same manager as if the ball had gone in."** A manager's entire evaluation flipped on a variable he did not control and that carried no information about his ability.

**The four lessons, in the order the book would give them:**
1. **The edge was real and it still lost.** Good process does not guarantee good outcomes over one trial; it pays out over many. Don't abandon a method because a single instance failed, and don't adopt one because a single instance worked.
2. **Judge by process over a large sample.** This is why the authors insist on 10,000-game databases, five-season minimums for managers, and goal difference over win percentage.
3. **Outcome bias is the industry's default error.** The sacking of Grant is the same error as crediting Ranieri with genius in 2016 and calling him finished in 2017 — and the same error as reading a manager's ability off a "low point" that was always going to regress to 1.3 points a game.
4. **Where the edges actually live**: static, repeatable, controllable situations (penalties, corners, free kicks), thin markets (lower leagues, unfashionable nationalities), and unfashionable roles (set-piece coaches, scouts) — not in the transfer market's headline signings.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

Apply these source-derived hypotheses at the scale their evidence supports. Historical percentages and averages belong to the book's sample and era; verify applicability before using them as a current baseline.
- **For sustained club performance, establish a resource baseline.** The book reports a strong long-run wage relationship in its historical samples. Check league, period, accounting basis, and timescale before applying it; use match evidence for a match-level claim.
- **Ask who decided before asking whether a signing was good.** Manager-led recruitment is the first named inefficiency: he spends without bearing risk and will likely be gone before the contract is.
- **Do not let a tournament carry a transfer decision by itself.** Test the small, highly visible sample against longer-run performance, role, fitness, wages, price, and alternatives. A tournament can add evidence without establishing the whole case.
- **Buy unfashionable nationalities and thin markets; sell into fashion.** Costa Rican fees rose tenfold in a year on one run to a shoot-out.
- **Concentrate the budget upward.** The second-best player's fee is the most decisive; the bottom five barely move results. And ask Anderson's question — "Who plays well with whom?" — rather than chasing a scarce superstar.
- **Check whether a "loss" is amortization.** A reported loss with heavy amortization is frequently an accounting event, not a cash event. Ask which one the claim rests on before repeating it.
- **Test the club's objective.** Win maximization is a useful source model; ownership constraints, cash needs, development goals, and profitability can change the decision. Do not infer motives from the model alone.
- **Separate the manager's contribution from resources and noise.** The book's historical estimates motivate a demanding evidence standard, not a universal five-season waiting rule. Combine role-specific evidence, recruitment influence, and a defensible counterfactual.
- **Test regression to the mean before crediting a sacking.** The source's 1.0-to-1.3 points-per-game pattern is a historical comparison. Check opposition, player availability, tactical changes, and an appropriate counterfactual before assigning a cause.
- **Distinguish the two markets when you see discrimination.** Transparent markets (players) purge it; opaque markets (managers, executives, scouts) preserve it indefinitely — so voluntary codes won't work there.
- **Knowledge gaps inside a league close fast.** An edge built on information (diet, scouting territory, analytics, set pieces) has a half-life. Ask how long this one has left.
- **Look for the static situations.** A third of goals come from set pieces, and they are the one phase you can both predict and control. Direct free kicks are usually worse than a pass.
- **Limit conclusions from small scouting samples.** Three live matches can contribute evidence but rarely settle a transfer decision. Combine broader video, data, and independent observations while checking their relevance and quality.
- **Before believing a country underperforms, price in home advantage, experience, income and population** — in that order of magnitude. Experience dominates; population barely matters.
- **In tournament football, expect noise.** Two or three one-goal games over two years separate "legendary" from "failure." Say so instead of inventing a cause.
- **Don't expect a World Cup to make anyone rich** — it may well make them happier, which is a different claim.
- **A club almost never dies**, which is why it can afford to be badly run. When something looks inexplicably irrational, check whether the penalty for irrationality actually exists.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Wages explain results; transfers explain very little.** That asymmetry is why the transfer market is where a well-run club can most easily gain, and it is where the named biases live.
2. **The transfer inefficiencies are systematic, not personal**: new-manager churn, post-tournament stars, marketing signings, fashionable nationalities, visual scouting bias, and money spread too evenly across the XI.
3. **Clubs are win-maximisers that almost never die** — which is simultaneously why they overspend, why they tolerate incompetence, and why they are stable enough for private equity to circle.
4. **Amortization is the accounting fact that makes headline "losses" misleading**; strip it out and big clubs are cash machines.
5. **At most ~10% of managers beat their wage bill.** The rest are figureheads, fuses and PR — which is also why discrimination survives in that market and not in the players'.
6. **Analytics is in its alchemy phase**; the demonstrable edges so far are in thin markets and set pieces (Brentford/Midtjylland), not in secret models.
7. **National-team expectations should be set by experience, home advantage, income and population** — in that order. England is a rich, experienced, medium-sized country ranked about where it should be, losing tournaments to arithmetic and to its own in-match exhaustion.
