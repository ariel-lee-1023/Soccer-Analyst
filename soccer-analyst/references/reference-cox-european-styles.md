# Zonal Marking: The Making of Modern European Football — Michael Cox
**Format**: pdf | **Pages**: ~430 | **Sections**: 19 chapters in 7 national parts + Introduction, 6 Transitions, Epilogue | **Depth**: study
**Use for**: modern pressing and positional-play vocabulary, national-style diagnosis, and the causal chain of who dominated Europe and why it ended

## Mental Model (read first)
Cox set out to analyse the playing styles of Europe's **seven most influential footballing countries** — Netherlands, Italy, France, Portugal, Spain, Germany, England — and discovered the book had to be chronological, because **the dominant style keeps changing hands**. So the structure is a relay: each nation gets a four-year window of dominance, and each **Transition** chapter explains how the baton passed.

> Voetbal (NL) 1992–96 → Calcio (Italy) 1996–2000 → Foot (France) 2000–04 → Futebol (Portugal) 2004–08 → Fútbol (Spain) 2008–12 → Fußball (Germany) 2012–16 → Football (England) 2016–20

**The definition of a "national style" is the most portable thing in the book.** It is not just the national team. It is: the approach of the dominant clubs, the nature of the star players, the philosophy of the coaches, what happens to that country's players when they move abroad, which imports succeed there, **how the referees officiate, and what the supporters cheer**. When you diagnose a style, check all of those — not the last international tournament.

1992 is the start date for three simultaneous reasons: the back-pass law, the rebranding of the European Cup as the Champions League, and the formation of the Premier League.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Part One — Voetbal (Netherlands), 1992–96

#### Ch 1 — Individual versus Collective
- **Core idea**: Dutch football's central argument is embodied by two men who could not stand each other — **Cruyff and Van Gaal** — and it is the same argument every football culture eventually has.
- **Cruyff**: expression, enjoyment, the individual inside a collective system. He personified Total Football while being "the only true individual in an otherwise very collective team," and won everything while insisting success mattered less than style.
- **Van Gaal**: "Football is a team sport, and the members of the team are therefore dependent upon each other. If certain players do not carry out their tasks properly on the pitch, then their colleagues will suffer." When Ajax lost, his complaint was that players "did not keep to the arrangement" — i.e. they broke their teammates' trust by improvising.
- **The correction Cox insists on**: Van Gaal's sides were **not** result-grinders. They were "extremely attack-minded, if mechanical." Van Gaal: "I suspect I'm fonder of playing the game well, rather than winning." **Mechanical ≠ negative.** Do not confuse system with caution.
- **Use it**: when a squad underperforms, ask whether the failure is of *arrangement* (players not doing their jobs) or of *permission* (players not allowed to solve problems). The two demand opposite fixes.

#### Ch 2 — Space (the most operational chapter for reading any modern match)
- **Core idea**: **Dutch football's unit of analysis is space, and everything else is derived from it.** Cox, via David Winner's *Brilliant Orange*, takes the geography seriously: 17% of the Netherlands should be underwater, only about half of it is more than a metre above sea level, and it is Europe's most densely populated major country. Its history is about creating space and then fighting over it.
- **Cruyff's formulation, the one to memorise**: "When you've got possession of the ball, you have to ensure that you have **as much space as possible**, and when you lose the ball you must **minimise the space your opponent has**. In fact, **everything in football is a function of distance**."
- **Cox's comparative point**: other nations organised their thinking around player attributes ("strong and fast"), event types ("win fifty-fifty balls"), or what to do with the ball ("get it forward quickly"). The Dutch organised it around **positioning and shape**. That is why the rest of Europe eventually copied them.
- **Michels' definition of Total Football** — two things, not one: **position-switching and pressing.** The press at Ajax came from Neeskens' aggressive man-marking of the opposing playmaker *combined with* Vasović pushing the line up to catch opponents offside. Michels: "The main aim of pressure football, 'the hunt', was regaining possession as soon as possible after the ball was lost in the opponents' half. The 'trapping' of the opponents is **only possible when all the lines are pushed up and play close together**."
- **The two coaching instructions that operationalise it** (Cruyff): "**the striker is the first defender**"; and "**the defenders determine the length of the playing area** … the distances between the banks of players can never be more than **10–15 metres**."
- **The 4-3-3 distinction Cox is careful about**: the modern interpretation (Guardiola) is one holder behind two — effectively **4-1-2-3**. The Dutch would often **flip the triangle into 4-2-1-3** and still call it 4-3-3, because to them 4-3-3 was "a philosophy as much as a system": a three-man attack spread across the pitch, when everyone else played boxy 4-4-2 or 5-3-2. **The midfield triangle's orientation — the difference between 4-3-3 and 4-2-3-1 in modern terms — was considered a minor detail.**
- **Anti-pattern**: judging a Dutch-lineage side by which way the triangle points rather than by whether the three-man attack holds width and the lines stay 10–15 metres apart.

#### Ch 3 — Playing Out from the Back
- **Core idea**: the Netherlands produced the modern goalkeeper because it had already **stopped thinking of the goalkeeper as a specialist**.
- **The founding decision**: for 1974, Cruyff pushed Michels to overlook the better shot-stoppers (Van Beveren, Schrijvers) for **Jan Jongbloed** — quick, comfortable sweeping behind the defence, good with his feet, one cap in twelve years. **Selection criteria set by the system, not by the position's traditional job description.**
- **The lineage**: Menzo (a converted sweeper — "I could play goalkeeper but I could also play football"), then **Frans Hoek**, the Netherlands' first goalkeeping coach, then **Van der Sar**, whom Jonathan Wilson calls "the first goalkeeper to operate as a genuine sweeper."
- **Van der Sar on 1992**: "The back-pass law changed my life, because I was already good with my feet." Hoek's list of what an Ajax keeper needed: understanding of the space around his goal, ability to play out to defenders, height and range, calmness — "a great foundation to build plays from" — and coachability.
- **The category to keep**: **"line-keepers"** — ball-stoppers who stay on their line — versus sweeper-keepers. The rest of Europe was still producing the former in 1992.
- **The honest complication**: Cruyff forgave Menzo's errors because his footballing ability compensated; **Van Gaal did not**, and dropped him after a UEFA Cup exit. Individual-versus-collective again, and pragmatism won.

*Transition (Netherlands → Italy)*: **Bosman**. The ruling that ended the Dutch model's edge by letting the richest leagues buy the players who executed it.

### Part Two — Calcio (Italy), 1996–2000

#### Ch 4 — Flexibility
- **Core idea, stated as sharply as anywhere in the book**: "Italian sides weren't about playing their natural game; **they were about stopping opponents from playing theirs**." Reactive, defensive-minded, tactically intelligent.
- **The worked incident**: 1996 Champions League final, Juventus v Ajax. Ajax switch wingers to attack Torricelli; Van Gaal brings on a fresh winger for extra time; Lippi has used all three substitutes — so he **swaps his full-backs**, Pessotto going right and Torricelli left. Problem solved with no substitution available.
  Cox's point is comparative: "You wouldn't have witnessed Brazil switching Cafu and Roberto Carlos, or Barcelona moving Ferrer to the left and Sergi to the right; it would have been unthinkable and fundamentally compromised their natural game."
- **The concept**: "Torricelli and Pessotto **weren't playing out of position, they were in another position they could play**." Elsewhere, a player with no clear best position is a weakness. In Serie A it was a virtue.
- **Who actually embodied the culture**: not Zidane or Del Piero but Torricelli (signed from amateur football for £20,000), Pessotto, Di Livio and Birindelli — none with meaningful Serie A experience on arrival. Lippi: "Every year we sold our best players, but the backbone of the squad stayed." Roy Keane on facing them: "tough, wily defenders, guys nobody's ever heard of, who closed space down, timed their tackles to perfection, were instinctively in the right cover positions and read the game superbly."
- **Use it**: **squad flexibility is a tactical asset with a cash value**, and it lives in the players nobody rates. Before calling a substitution "forced," ask what positional switches were available inside the XI.

#### Ch 5 — The Third Attacker
- **Core idea**: the *trequartista* — a number 10 behind two strikers, around whom the team is built — was under threat from Sacchi's pressing 4-4-2, and **Roberto Baggio is the case file** for the system-versus-genius argument.
- **Baggio's manifesto**, which is exactly the anti-Sacchi position: "I'm a ball-player, and I think it's better to have ten disorganised footballers than ten organised runners." And on being benched: "**I feel like a Ferrari being driven by a traffic warden.** A coach must, above all, be a good psychologist. If he imposes his demands harshly, he suffocates the personalities and creativity of his players."
- **Cox's verdict is deliberately split**: substituting Baggio after Pagliuca's red card in 1994 "was probably the right decision" — and yet Italian opinion concluded the country had reached the final "because of Baggio's brilliance rather than Sacchi's tactics." **A correct decision can still lose the argument, and the argument then shapes the next decade of selection.**
- **Use it**: when a creator is dropped, separate the tactical case from the cultural verdict. They rarely agree, and the cultural verdict is what changes recruitment.

#### Ch 6 — Catenaccio
- **Definition, kept exact**: *catenaccio* ("door bolt") is not a synonym for negative football. It refers to **a specific tactic — a libero/sweeper playing behind stopper centre-backs who man-mark opposition forwards.** The spare defender "inevitably meant a shortfall in more advanced positions, which generally produced defensive football." (The same trade Wilson names: a spare man here is a missing man there.)
- **Herrera's defence of himself**: his catenaccio was "simply about removing a midfielder to introduce an extra defender," which liberated Facchetti to attack from left-back; others "copied the sweeper without adjusting the positional responsibilities of the defenders," which is what made catenaccio more defensive than he intended.
- **Rocco, who never denied it**, with the best motto in the book: "**Let's hope it's a good game, but that the better team doesn't win.**" He was content to be overrun in midfield to secure the back.
- **The rebrand**: as liberos became better in possession and pushed into midfield, the system was renamed ***il gioco all'italiana*** at home while "catenaccio" stuck abroad.
- **Trapattoni's typology, which is still useful for scouting**: a **libero** is about "being able to read the play and anticipate the situation"; a **stopper** requires "physical strength, aerial power, determined tackling skills and intelligent aggression." Italy admired both — Scirea and Gentile, "beauty and beast respectively."
- **What the era's real fight was about**: zonal versus man-marking, and Cox's key observation is that the argument "wasn't merely about which approach was most effective, but also about **which approach was most Italian**." **Tactical debates are identity debates wearing tactical clothes.** Watch for this in any club with a strong tradition.

*Transition (Italy → France)*: Serie A's money and its owners begin to unravel; France's academy system starts producing the players everyone else buys.

### Part Three — Foot (France), 2000–04

#### Ch 7 — Speed
- **Core idea**: France won a World Cup and a European Championship two years apart with **nine of the same starting XI** — and a completely different identity. "In 1998 France tasted success by playing like an Italian side, but in 2000 they added style and created a true French footballing identity."
- **The two changes that did it**: Guivarc'h (who scored no goals in the 1998 triumph) replaced by **two lightning-quick centre-forwards, Henry and Anelka**; and Karembeu dropped — "France's shift from three cautious midfielders to two, and from three attacking weapons to four."
- **The national managerial type, and it is a real category**: "Whereas Dutch coaches lectured about their philosophy and Italian coaches were studious tacticians, French coaches were, appropriately, more laissez-faire. They weren't ideologists and they weren't grandmasters. Instead they had **the mentality of youth coaches**, and believed their fundamental job was to guide players and help them fulfil their potential." Jacquet, Lemerre, Houllier (a former schoolteacher who spent three years with the U18s and U20s coaching Anelka, Henry and Trezeguet), and Wenger: "**I am, first and foremost, an educator.**"
- **Use it**: three legitimate manager archetypes — ideologist, strategist, educator. Match the archetype to the club's actual problem before judging the appointment.

#### Ch 8 — The Number 10
- **Core idea**: French football's post-war history is a fight between two coaching ideologies, and **Zidane is what happens when the second one wins**.
  - **Georges Boulogne** (from 1958, author of the coaching curriculum, later the first technical director): thought French football placed too much emphasis on enjoyment; his words were "organisation," "effort," "productivity." He failed as national coach — but **he built the youth academies**, which is why he still matters.
  - **Albert Batteux** (the most successful manager in French history): quick passing, direct dribbling, individual brilliance, and the motto "**Plan the game so the talent will flourish.**"
- **The lineage**: Batteux → Kopa as a number 10 → Hidalgo (Batteux's winger, later national coach) → Platini → Zidane. Zidane spent five years each at Platini's Juventus and Kopa's Real Madrid, and was directed to Platini's old peg on arrival at Juve. He resisted the burden: "People have to understand I will never be Platini. I am not a leader of men and never will be."
- **Houllier's framing of the trade-off** is the reusable line: "You must be careful not to stereotype players, not to lose their charm and talent. You must have teamwork, and you can have a sophisticated system, but **one outstanding player for one moment can bring an added quality**."

#### Ch 9 — The Water Carrier
- **Core idea, and the book's best analogy**: French sides are structured like **professional cycling teams** — a designated leader plus *domestiques* who sacrifice their own game for his: pacing, leading out, ferrying water bottles.
- **The comparative claim, which is a ready-made diagnostic grid**: "**No other country so unashamedly supports their star player with a series of unfashionable, limited, no-nonsense holding players behind.** In Spain, central midfielders are creators, in England they're expected to cover lots of ground, in Italy they're versatile and capable of playing in wider positions. In France, the holding midfielders play purely functional roles, always thinking about their star man."
- **The historical arc**: the 1984 "magic square" (Platini, Tigana, Giresse, Fernández) had no pure holder — even the deepest man was a deep-lying playmaker. By 1998–2000 the structure had polarised around **Deschamps**, patronised as limited but one of only three men to win a World Cup as both player and manager (with Zagallo and Beckenbauer).
- **Use it**: before calling a holding midfielder "limited," identify whose game he is subsidising and price that in.

*Transition (France → Portugal)*: Portugal's coaching school and its golden generation reach the top at the same moment as Mourinho.

### Part Four — Futebol (Portugal), 2004–08

#### Ch 10 — Structure
- **Core idea**: Porto's 2004 Champions League win is the underdog result of the era, and **the Mourinho of that period is not the Mourinho of legend**: "Mourinho would later become renowned as a defensive coach, but during his period in Portugal he was committed to **high-tempo, proactive possession play**."
- **The season's shape**: 2003/04 was "arguably the competition's greatest season, because it was completely dominated by underdogs" — and Cox rejects the lazy reading that this was a weak year: it was Arsenal's Invincible season, Ancelotti's Milan's only Serie A title, Lyon at their strongest. All four quarter-finals produced upsets; all four semi-finalists were outsiders.
- **Mourinho's own account of his apprenticeship**, which is the transferable part: under Bobby Robson (a hands-on attacking training-ground coach) his job was to plan the training practices — "**Whilst maintaining the primacy of attacking football, I tried to organise it better, and this organisation stems directly from the defence.**" Under Van Gaal, who planned and delegated, he learned the opposite half of the job.
- **Modest ambition, correctly scoped**: Mourinho's two stated targets were to reach the knockout stage and beat one big club — which is why he celebrated the Manchester United win more than the final.

#### Ch 11 — The First Port of Call
- **Core idea**: the Portuguese coaching school's contribution is **the transition as an object of study**, and **Deco** is its first complete product.
- **The four-phase model, which the whole modern coaching vocabulary rests on**: football is "a series of four situations, flowing continuously in a circle: **the possession phase; the transition from possession to out-of-possession; the out-of-possession phase; and then the transition to possession.** And repeat."
- **Deco as the profile**: could play as a 10 in a 4-3-1-2 or as a left-centre midfielder in a 4-3-3; renowned for receiving in tight areas with his head up and pausing before an incisive pass — *and* for work rate, aggressive pressing and tracking back (15 bookings in 30 league games). Mourinho: "Not only does he produce great individual brilliance, he offers so much more to the team."
- **Roxburgh's forecast, quoted here as in Wilson**: a strikerless side needs "six Decos in midfield" — all able to rotate, attack and defend. **Universality named as a recruitment target.**
- **The uncomfortable subplot Cox does not skip**: Deco's Portuguese citizenship split the golden generation (Rui Costa and Figo objected; Mourinho, who made him, objected too — "Portugal is for the Portuguese"), while Scolari, himself Brazilian, insisted. **Identity fights inside a squad are a tactical variable.**

#### Ch 12 — Wingers
- **Core idea**: a nation with a genuine style always has **a cliché attached to it**, and Portugal's was self-authored — great football, no clinical striker.
- **Carlos Queiroz** is the intellectual source: a modest goalkeeper from Mozambique who studied at the Technical University of Lisbon and in 1982 became **the first student anywhere to graduate with a master's in football training methodology** ("This was almost a revolution… with that I opened many possibilities in the intellectual field as a coach"). He won the U20 World Cup in 1989 and 1991, and **believed top-level football would become strikerless** — the centre-forward dropping deep, joining build-up, dragging defenders out to create space for others.
- **The proof it was doctrine, not accident**: Portugal's own stars complained about it for a decade. Figo: "Portuguese football doesn't seem to produce out-and-out strikers any more… Domingos and João Pinto like to drop back, get involved in the build-up and run at defences from deep." Even Nuno Gomes, who scored four at Euro 2000, described his own movement as designed to disrupt rather than to poach: "That's the way I help the team."
- **Use it**: when a country or club repeatedly "fails" to produce one position, check whether its coaching curriculum stopped asking for it.

*Transition (Portugal → Spain)*: the players Portugal and Argentina exported meet the academy that had been quietly training them all.

### Part Five — Fútbol (Spain), 2008–12

#### Ch 13 — Tiki-taka
- **Core idea**: Guardiola inherited **a generation of midfielders who had been taught to play like him** — Xavi and Iniesta were instructed at La Masia to watch Barcelona's number 4. The revolution was pre-loaded; the appointment detonated it.
- **The founding selection decision, again from Cruyff**: he insisted on "**a methodical playmaker, rather than a defensive ball-winner, at the base of midfield**," and pulled Guardiola out of the reserves to do it. Van Gaal: even as a player Guardiola "could speak like a coach."
- **Guardiola's own wilderness years as evidence**: pushed out at 30 because "players like me have become extinct… To play in front of the defence right now, you have to be a ball-winner. If I were a 20-year-old at Barcelona, I'd never make it." He then learned defensive positioning from Capello at Roma and studied under **Juanma Lillo** in Mexico — "sometimes regarded as the inventor of the 4-2-3-1 formation, and an inspiration for many Spanish coaches."
- **What Guardiola actually changed at Barcelona**: an aggressive sweeper-keeper, an advanced defensive line, integrated pressing, and later no conventional centre-forward — "**More than anything else, however, Guardiola was concerned with dominating midfield through possession play.**"
- **The decision that made it work** was a personnel one everyone said was impossible: playing **Xavi and Iniesta and Busquets together** ("It wasn't Xavi or Iniesta, it was Xavi and Iniesta. Oh, and Busquets too") against the orthodoxy that a midfield three needs three distinct roles. Barcelona clicked in game three, 6–1 at Sporting Gijón, and took 58 points from the next 20 games.
- **Xavi as the profile**: nicknamed "The Machine" for his brain, not his body. "I am, basically, a passer. I get the ball, I pass the ball." His actual skill was **positioning himself to drag opponents out of position before prodding the ball past them** — i.e. possession as a space-manipulation device, not as ball-hogging.
- **The summarising line**: Guardiola "had faith in both the midfield (the zone of the pitch) and in midfielders (the type of player)." Keep those two claims separate when you assess any possession side.

#### Ch 14 — False 9s & Argentines
- **Core idea**: Spain's golden era was staffed and philosophised partly by **Argentines** — Valdano as Spanish football's "grand philosopher," Menotti as the ideologue of attacking football, Maradona at Barça, and every candidate "new Maradona" (Ortega, Aimar, Saviola, Riquelme) imported into La Liga.
- **The vocabulary, kept exact**: the ***enganche*** ("hook"), revered in Argentina more than anywhere; the ***pibe*** — "a scruffy, lower-class kid who developed his skills playing street football and refused to allow his natural instincts to be compromised by coaches," ideally physically underdeveloped, a brilliant dribbler, left-footed. Maradona "didn't create the stereotype but fulfilled it perfectly."
- **Messi as the hinge**: signed at 13 on a napkin by Rexach because Newell's wouldn't fund his growth-hormone treatment; raised outside La Masia with his family, in an Argentine community, with Latin American teammates. **He belonged to Barcelona's system and Argentina's tradition at once**, which is why the false nine reads as both.

#### Ch 15 — El Clásico
- **Core idea**: the defining strategic response of the era was Real Madrid's, and it cost them their identity. Barça and Real are always "on the two sides of the scale" (Xavi) — so when Barcelona owned technical football, **Real answered with strength, speed and physicality: "In moving away from technical football, Spain's biggest club became less Spanish."**
- **The morale collapse, documented**: Schuster before a Camp Nou trip — "Winning at Camp Nou is impossible… it's their year." Cox's verdict: "describing a must-win game as a cannot-win game was suicidal." Sacked.
- **The tactical worked example — the 2–6 at the Bernabéu.** Juande Ramos matched Guardiola's midfield triangle with a 4-2-3-1 and **effectively fielded two left-backs**: Heinze, a converted centre-back, to track Messi, and Marcelo as a defensive-minded winger to stop Dani Alves overlapping. It was a coherent plan against the previous game's problem. Then Messi played as a **false nine** — his first such deployment since Guardiola's debut win — and the marking scheme had nobody assigned to where he stood. He scored twice.
  **The lesson**: a man-oriented plan is a bet that the opponent's players will occupy their listed positions. The false nine is precisely the move that voids the bet.

*Transition (Spain → Germany)*: the counter to possession arrives from the league that had been studying transitions all along.

### Part Six — Fußball (Germany), 2012–16

#### Ch 16 — Verticality
- **Core idea**: Germany did not oppose the Spanish model — it **absorbed it and added penetration**. Löw: "**Spain are always our role model.** I am totally convinced that one can no longer win titles by playing ugly football." Kroos, per Xavi, was "like my successor on the field."
- **The distinction that matters**: Xavi's Barcelona would "**noticeably decline counter-attacking opportunities** when they won the ball, preferring to retain possession," and Guardiola spoke of a **"15-pass rule"** — that many passes were needed for his players to reorganise into the correct positional structure. German football was about **transitions**.
- **The number worth carrying**: per 100 games between 2012 and 2016, Opta counted roughly **32 fast-break goals in the Bundesliga against 19 in La Liga** — and that undercounts, because it excludes set-pieces won from counters and goals from pressing after an initial counter.
- **The worked example — Bayern 7–0 Barcelona on aggregate, 2013.** Bayern had Europe's second-best ball-retention numbers that season, and **deliberately declined to compete for possession**: Barcelona had 63% in Munich and 57% in Spain. Bayern instead attacked "the areas where Barcelona were stereotypically, if rarely, exposed: **physicality, set-pieces and counter-attacking**." Two of the first goals came from set-pieces. "Bayern were capable of outpassing almost anyone, but against Barcelona they were harder, better, faster, stronger."
  **The transferable move**: against a side that is best at one thing, **do not contest that thing** — contest the three areas where they are merely adequate. Being an all-rounder beats being a specialist in a knockout tie.

#### Ch 17 — Gegenpressing (the most operationally useful chapter in the book)
- **The tradition it broke from**: Germany was "the only country more fixated than Italy on strict man-marking and a sweeper," and for decades its best player *was* the sweeper — Beckenbauer, then Matthäus, Sammer (Ballon d'Or 1996), Nowotny, Ramelow; even Ballack started there. Roy Hodgson in 1997: strip the language and the kit from two teams and you couldn't tell English from Italian from French — "the league that differs from all the others is the German league." **Germany's distinctiveness in the 1990s was also its backwardness.**
- **Where the idea came from**: Klopp watched Barcelona and, unlike everyone else, "studied what they did **the moment they lost it**." Focusing on immediate regain "short-circuits" the four-phase cycle — the defensive transition now leads straight back into possession. "Sometimes you could watch Barcelona for an entire half and not witness their out-of-possession structure."
- **The definition, exactly as Cox insists on it**: "**Counter-pressing isn't to pressing what counter-attacking is to attacking. It's not necessarily countering a press, but pressing a counter.**"
- **The two differences between Guardiola's and Klopp's pressing — this is the distinction to actually use when reading a match**:
  1. **Motive.** Guardiola pressed *defensively*: "Without the ball, we are a disastrous team, a horrible team, so we need the ball." It was a proactive way of avoiding having to defend, and it ended in a long possession phase. Klopp pressed *offensively*: "Gegenpressing lets you win back the ball nearer to the goal. It's only one pass away from a really good opportunity. **No playmaker in the world can be as good as a good gegenpressing situation.**" Same action, opposite purpose.
  2. **Method.** Guardiola's players pressed "intelligently, with one man pressuring the opponent on the ball and others cutting off passing angles." Klopp's were "less calculating and more extreme — they piled players into the immediate vicinity of the player in possession, almost crowding him out through intimidation."
- **The structural consequence, which is how you spot it from the stands**: Klopp's wide midfielders (Großkreutz, Błaszczykowski) **tucked inside**. "While other teams were focusing on **vertical compactness**, Klopp was also concerned with **horizontal compactness**," and Dortmund regularly won the ball by **boxing opponents in towards the touchline.**
- **The block, and why it was not a full-pitch press**: Dortmund typically used a **medium block, standing off the centre-backs**, and pressed once the ball went into full-backs or midfielders. "Dortmund rarely used a full-pitch press, because doing so encouraged the opposition to kick the ball long… Klopp really wanted the opposition to **play their way into trouble**."
  **Diagnostic use**: a team that stands off the centre-backs is not being passive. It is choosing where it wants the ball to arrive.
- **The illustrating goal**: Bayern goal-kick, Badstuber to Schweinsteiger, immediately pressured on both sides by Großkreutz and Lewandowski; Schweinsteiger panics, misses the ball entirely, Großkreutz drives forward and sets up Barrios. "If Germany's most revered midfielder couldn't cope with Dortmund's energy, what chance did other Bundesliga players have?"

#### Ch 18 — Reinvention
- **Core idea**: Germany 2014 was "an egalitarian, harmonious group" with no single hero — and its six candidate heroes (Götze, Lahm, Schweinsteiger, Kroos, Müller, Neuer) shared two things: **they were all Bayern players, and they were all masters of positional reinvention.**
- **The Guardiola statistic worth keeping**: six of Spain's 2010 World Cup final XI had played for Guardiola's Barcelona the previous season; **six of Germany's 2014 final XI had played for Guardiola's Bayern the previous season.** Two consecutive World Cups shaped by one club coach.
- **The mechanism, and it is a real constraint on international management**: Löw "was an astute tactician… but limited time on the training ground meant he **couldn't transform players** in the manner of a club manager." Guardiola, winning the Bundesliga at a canter, could afford ambitious experiments — and the national side inherited players "suddenly comfortable in an entirely different position."
  **Use it**: a national team's tactical options are largely a function of what its dominant club has been experimenting with. Look at the club, then predict the country.

*Transition (Germany → England)*: Europe's best coaches all end up in one league.

### Part Seven — Football (England), 2016–20

#### Ch 19 — The Mixer
- **Core idea**: even as the Premier League became the coaching capital of Europe, a recognisably English counter-current persisted — Allardyce, Pulis, **Dyche** and **Warnock** — and their role "involved protesting against the increasing popularity of possession play and complaining about any attempt to clamp down on hard tackling."
- **The rhetoric, preserved**: Dyche after beating Liverpool — "It was a myth that came out about five years ago that possession wins you games." Warnock — "Do you want to see shots on target or do you want us to pass the ball across the pitch on the halfway line?" (Cardiff were promoted with a **57% pass-completion rate**.)
- **Cox's four-part causal explanation of English directness** — the most useful part of the chapter, because it is a template for explaining any national style:
  1. **Climate.** Of the seven nations, England is the rainiest and windiest, making the run of the ball unpredictable and pitches muddy. Premier League surfaces are immaculate now, but **grass-roots surfaces are where players are formed**.
  2. **Rugby.** A full-contact neighbour sport whose supporters emphasise toughness, leaving football "compelled to prove its machismo by fetishising hard tackles."
  3. **Class.** Rugby union became the private-school winter sport, so football was "considered a purely working-class sport… a pursuit unworthy of academic or scientific attention." Hence "**a deep-rooted anti-intellectualism**," and managers evaluated on motivation and man-management rather than tactics.
  4. **Charles Hughes.** Selective statistics, POMO, "get it in the mixer." Cox's fair-minded caveat: Hughes' theories "were arguably taken too literally… although many still have merit; he emphasised, for example, **the importance of pressing**."

### Epilogue — does national identity still matter?
- **The case against**: Zidane's Real Madrid won three straight Champions Leagues (2016–18) after 25 years of nobody retaining it, with **no decipherable national identity** — a Costa Rican keeper, a Spanish/French/Brazilian defence, a Brazilian-Croatian-German midfield, Portuguese, French, Welsh and Spanish attackers. "Possibly the most cosmopolitan top-class side of the modern era," and its fans did not complain.
- **The case for**: "Following football isn't solely about winning trophies." Supporters want a style "that befits the club's traditions — an identity that has clearly been influenced by the country in which they play." Even Cruyff, modern football's most dogmatic philosopher: "The way to improve is not by copying what happens in Spain or Italy, but by looking first at yourself… **You play football the way the public wants you to.**"
- **And the institutional backing**: UEFA states a belief in "preserving football's local, regional and national identities."
- **Cox's closing reason for caring**: footballing style is, for millions, "the best opportunity to discover more about their neighbours… a window into other cultures."

## Worked Example
**Bayern Munich 4–0 and 3–0 Barcelona, Champions League semi-final, 2013 — how to beat the best team at something without competing at it.**

Barcelona had not fallen below 50% possession in over 300 games — since before Guardiola's appointment. Bayern that season had Europe's second-best retention numbers, so the pre-match question was whether they would try to out-pass Barcelona.

They didn't even attempt it. Barcelona took 63% of the ball in Munich and 57% in the return, and lost 7–0 on aggregate.

What Bayern did instead was attack the three areas where Barcelona were adequate rather than exceptional: **physicality, set-pieces and counter-attacking.** The first two goals in Munich came from set-pieces, finished by Müller and Gómez — the kind of goal Barcelona's structure was never optimised to prevent, and which possession statistics cannot deter. And Germany's structural advantage was already measured: over this period the Bundesliga produced roughly 32 fast-break goals per 100 games against La Liga's 19, because Guardiola's model deliberately *declined* counter-attacks in favour of the 15 passes needed to reorganise into positional structure.

**Three transferable lessons:**
1. **Do not contest an opponent's best phase.** Barcelona's possession was not beatable; it was avoidable.
2. **A specialist loses to an all-rounder over two legs.** Bayern "didn't perfect one particular style as gracefully as Barcelona, but they were true all-rounders, capable of winning matches in different ways."
3. **A philosophy's strength names its exposure.** The 15-pass rule is what made Barcelona's structure stable — and it is exactly what made them slow to counter and vulnerable to a side that turned every turnover into a sprint.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- **Triangulate a national or club style.** Relevant evidence can include national and club teams, player profiles, coaching methods, migration, refereeing, and spectator expectations. Use the sources needed to test the claim; this is not a mandatory six-source checklist.
- **Judge distances, not effort.** "Everything in football is a function of distance." Ask how far apart the banks of players are (Cruyff's working number: 10–15 metres) before you ask who ran more.
- **Check the support behind a press.** Inspect gaps between lines and the opponent's available exits. A high first line with deeper support may leave exploitable space; assess the intended trap and observed outcomes before calling it uncoordinated.
- **Separate a press's motive from its method.** Guardiola pressed to avoid defending; Klopp pressed to attack. The same action, and completely different expectations about what should happen in the next five seconds.
- **Ask whether a team is vertically or horizontally compact.** Klopp's inside-tucking wide midfielders are the tell for a side that intends to box you in against the touchline.
- **A medium block that stands off the centre-backs is a choice, not passivity.** A full-pitch press invites the long ball; standing off invites the opponent to play his way into trouble. Name which one you're watching.
- **Against a dominant specialist, contest the phases they're merely adequate at** — set-pieces, physical duels, transitions — rather than the one they've perfected.
- **A man-oriented plan is a bet on where opponents will stand.** The false nine, the inverted winger and the inverted full-back exist to void that bet. When a marking scheme has nobody assigned to a zone, that is the whole story of the match.
- **Every spare man is borrowed from somewhere.** Catenaccio's libero produced a shortfall further forward, which is what made it defensive. Find the shortfall before you praise the overload.
- **"Mechanical" is not "negative."** Van Gaal's Ajax and Sacchi's Milan were both highly systematised and highly attacking. Judge intent by where they win the ball and how quickly they attack, not by how drilled they look.
- **Squad flexibility is a tactical asset held by unfashionable players.** Before calling a substitution forced, ask which positional switches were available within the XI.
- **When a country stops producing a position, look at its coaching curriculum before its gene pool.** Portugal stopped producing strikers because Queiroz's doctrine stopped asking for one.
- **Match the manager archetype to the problem**: ideologist (installs a philosophy), strategist (wins specific matches), educator (develops players). Hiring the wrong one for the job is the most common avoidable error in the book.
- **A national team's ceiling is set by what its dominant club has been experimenting with** — national coaches don't have the training time to reinvent players.
- **Treat tactical arguments inside a club as identity arguments.** Zonal versus man-marking in 1990s Italy was about what it meant to be Italian. Expect the same energy wherever a tradition is strong.
- **A pre-match admission of inferiority is a tactical event.** Schuster called an unwinnable game unwinnable and was sacked before he could coach it.

## Key Takeaways
1. **The dominant style in Europe changes hands roughly every four years, and each new one is an answer to the last.** Voetbal → Calcio → Foot → Futebol → Fútbol → Fußball → Football.
2. **Space is the master variable**: as much as possible with the ball, as little as possible without it, everything a function of distance. Pressing is only viable when all the lines push up together.
3. **Counter-pressing is "pressing a counter," not "countering a press"** — and its two live variants differ in motive (defensive vs attacking) and method (cut passing angles vs swarm the ball).
4. **Horizontal compactness is as much a design choice as vertical compactness**, and it shows up in where the wide players stand.
5. **Catenaccio names a specific mechanism** — libero behind man-marking stoppers — and its cost, a shortfall further forward, is the general law that every spare man is borrowed.
6. **The four-phase cycle** (possession → transition out → out of possession → transition in) is the shared vocabulary; the innovations of the last twenty years are almost all attempts to short-circuit one of the two transitions.
7. **Style is produced by conditions — climate, pitches, class, refereeing, crowds, coaching curricula — and only then by coaches.** When a country lacks a position or a habit, check the curriculum before the character.
