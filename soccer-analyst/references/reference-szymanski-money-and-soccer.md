# Money and Soccer: A Soccernomics Guide — Stefan Szymanski
**Format**: pdf | **Pages**: ~290 | **Sections**: 11 (Dominance & Distress, Players, Stadiums, Revenue, Debt, Ownership, Strategy, Insolvency, America, Regulation, Conclusion) | **Depth**: study
**Use for**: what a club's finances *permit*, before you ask what its manager *chose* — and for reading any claim about crisis, dominance, debt or "unfair" spending

## Mental Model (read first)
The book's thesis fits in one sentence: **dominance and distress are the same phenomenon, and both are produced by promotion and relegation.**

> "Dominance is the product of the promotion-and-relegation system, which ensures that there is an open, competitive market. It may seem counterintuitive at first, but **open competition with unlimited freedom of entry has created a system in which only a few clubs win consistently.**"

The mechanism: an open league has no barrier to entry, so competition drives expected profit at every league position down toward zero. Money buys success (the player market works), success generates revenue, and dominant clubs reinvest their winnings into ever-better squads — which is itself the barrier smaller clubs face. Everyone else "must either risk insolvency or find a sugar daddy."

Szymanski's four summary facts about the industry:
1. **A few clubs dominate.**
2. **The rest survive on the edge of financial distress.**
3. **Regulation has been "light touch."**
4. **The game has grown dramatically anyway.**

And his governing analytic move — the one to copy — is **the distinction between individual crisis and systemic crisis**: "You don't treat the common cold in the same way that you treat a heart attack." Clubs constantly go insolvent and almost never die, so persistent distress is not evidence of a failing system.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1 — Dominance and Distress
- **Core idea**: dominance is universal and it is not new. Real Madrid, HB Tórshavn of the Faroe Islands (population 50,000) and Jeunesse Esch of Luxembourg have **the same relationship to their leagues**, despite squad values of €600m, €0.5m and €1.5m respectively. "Large or small, the relationship of these clubs to the other teams in their domestic leagues is the same — they are dominant."
- **The numbers**: across twenty European leagues over fifty years, **the dominant club won about a third of all titles**, and the average number of different champions was **ten**. Portugal had four; the Netherlands, Scotland and Turkey five. **There is no relationship between league size or wealth and the degree of dominance** — "Germany… looks rather like tiny Luxembourg or the Faroe Islands."
- **The benchmark that makes this striking**: in an open pyramid, if every team were equally strong you would expect roughly **forty different champions over fifty years**. The observed average is ten.
- **The myth killed**: that commercialisation created dominance. Comparing 1964–89 with 1989–2014, the pattern is "almost indistinguishable" — the Champions League had eight winning nations in each quarter-century. Across nineteen leagues, titles won by the most successful team rose 9% and the number of different champions fell 9%. "**If dominance increased, then surely not by that much.**"
- **Use it**: when someone says money has ruined competitive balance, ask for the pre-money baseline. There usually wasn't one.

### 2 — Players
- **Core idea**: "**Every game is a gamble. Or an investment. Or both.**" All the commitments — wages, fees, academy costs — are made *before* the game, while the outcome remains uncertain. Anderson and Sally, quoted: "half of all goals can be attributed to luck, and the better team wins only half of the time." **The main activity of a professional club is the acquisition of playing talent**, and that makes it a form of financial speculation.
- **The scale of the decision problem**: a Premier League manager makes **418 starting-XI decisions and 684 total selections** across 38 games, from a 25-man squad (United employed 82 players across 2013). Ferguson made **270 changes to his starting line-up** in 2012/13 — seven per game.
- **Individual performance is wildly variable.** Bundesliga expert ratings (2005–2010, 845 players): mean 3.62, standard deviation 0.76 — so two-thirds of the time a player rates between 2.86 and 4.38, a huge spread. And because team performance is **multiplicative rather than additive** (one player's good game raises his teammates'), "under plausible scenarios, the best performance of the team could easily be **twenty times better than its worst**."
- **The central empirical result — wages versus bookmakers.** Szymanski built the most simplistic model imaginable: *the team that spends more on players is more likely to win*, with no account of managers, injuries, form, psychology or morale. Correlations with Bet365's odds for the 2012/13 Premier League: **+0.83 (win), +0.78 (tie), +0.83 (loss)**. "Not perfect, but extremely close."
- **And the limit, stated just as firmly**: "when it comes to single games, the model does not perform a whole lot better than a blind guess." In 2012/13 **the more expensive team failed to win more than 50% of the time** — but it was still more than twice as likely to win than to lose.
- **The law of large numbers is the whole mechanism.** A 2:1 favourite wins a best-of-three 74% of the time, a best-of-five 79%, a best-of-seven 83%. "**Any advantage for one team gets amplified by repetition.**" Over 38 games, probability becomes near-certainty.
- **The record**: over twenty Premier League seasons, the **highest wage bill won nine titles, the second-highest seven, the third-highest three**, and Blackburn won once with the fourth-highest — "no team paying less than this has won the Premier League since then." UEFA's own study of 52 European leagues: highest wage bill wins **56%** of titles, second-highest 21%, third-highest 8%, everyone else 15%.
- **Cups are only slightly more random.** Over twenty years the FA Cup went to the highest spender seven times, second five, third twice, fourth three times — only Wigan (20th in spending) was an outlier. "The law of large numbers will not work for any one team in a cup competition, but it tends to work in favor of the strong teams **collectively**."
- **Correlation is not causation — and Szymanski says so himself** (Box 2.1). Reverse causation is possible (success generates money that gets paid out to players). His defence is theoretical rather than statistical: the player market has many buyers and sellers, abundant public information and enormous scrutiny — the textbook conditions for a competitive market — so better players *should* command higher wages. **The crucial caveat**: "this is **not** an argument about incentives — you cannot turn donkeys into superstars by paying them more money. But a bigger checkbook will get you access to the superstars."
- **The manager as fund manager** — the chapter's best analogy. Stock markets are well-informed; thousands of experts price risk; almost no fund manager beats the market persistently. "The same reasoning applies quite closely to soccer." A manager who buys a balanced portfolio of talent will perform in line with what he spent.
- **Historical context that explains why the wage–position link is recent** (Box 2.2): a maximum wage until 1961, no movement without club permission until 1963, no foreign players in England until 1978, and clubs colluding to keep wages down. The market only became efficient after Bosman (1995), by which point 36% of players across 31 European leagues were foreign.

### 3 — Stadiums
- **Core idea**: the stadium is the club's other long-term investment (≈40-year life against a 3-year player contract), and its **size sets a ceiling on the club's potential revenue** — but building one is a genuine bet.
- **"If you build it they will come" — sometimes.** Szymanski and Bastien Drut studied six World Cups and four European Championships and found domestic top-division attendance rose **15–20% in the host country, an effect lasting at least five years**, concentrated at the stadiums that hosted matches. The US baseball analogue: a **32–37% "honeymoon effect"** in a new stadium's opening year, persisting six to ten seasons.
- **The counter-case that keeps it honest**: South Africa 2010 built five stadiums averaging ~50,000 capacity; average Premier Soccer League attendance afterwards was **6,722, lower than the 7,639 before the tournament**. "They have built them, but no one has come… in this case, **supply actually seems to be destroying demand**" — because an empty stadium is itself off-putting.
- **The capacity ladder**: Premier League ~37,000 average capacity, Championship 26,000, League One 16,000, League Two 11,000 — and the Premier League also fills **over 90%** of its seats. Capacity and utilisation both scale with success, which is why stadium size is simultaneously a cause and a consequence.

### 4 — Revenue
- **The parable that opens it — ITV Digital.** ONdigital agreed to pay £315m for Football League rights, equal to the total annual revenue of all 72 clubs. In the two years *before* the contract took effect, Division One wage spending rose **36% then 24% — two-thirds in total** — against 10% actual revenue growth. Eight months into the deal ITV Digital collapsed, the parent-company guarantees turned out never to have been **signed**, and a $320m hole opened. Nine clubs entered administration in 2002 and eight in 2003.
  **But the lesson Szymanski draws is not "don't count your chickens."** It is that **all business runs on credit**: "expenditure goes before revenue… Farmers plant seeds, harvest, and then sell." Clubs must buy a squad before they can sell tickets.
- **The growth record**: average top-division revenue went from £127,000 in 1958 to £126m in 2013 — **almost fiftyfold after inflation**, a compound 7.3% a year. Second and third tiers grew 4.6% a year (twelvefold); the fourth tier 2.6% (fourfold). UK real incomes over the same period grew 2.2% a year. **Even the fourth tier outgrew the national economy.**
- **Football is largely decoupled from the business cycle** (correlation with the wider economy just **+0.12**). The 2008–09 recession shrank the economy 6% while Premier League revenues grew 20% then 9%. What actually moves revenue is **new broadcasting contracts** — four of the nine biggest annual increases coincided with one, and one of the worst years (2005) was a good economic year with a contract worth 7% less.
- **Inequality: between divisions, not within them.** In the early 1960s each division earned about 50% more than the one below. Between the top tier and the second tier, that gap reached three-to-one by 1985 and about **seven-to-one** today. But **within** a division, dispersion roughly doubled between 1958 and 1990 and has barely moved since. **The gap that grew is the promotion/relegation cliff, not the gap between first and last in the same league** — which is why relegation is now such a violent financial event.

### 5 — Debt
- **The equilibrium claim, and it is the book's economic backbone**: "for every position in the league, **the level of spending required to achieve that position is almost exactly matched by the revenues that can be earned**." At every position, expected profit must be roughly the same and roughly zero — because if a higher position were more profitable, lower clubs would bid for the players until it wasn't; and if lower positions were profitable, new clubs would enter from below. **Promotion and relegation is what keeps entry open and therefore keeps profits at zero.**
  With one soccer-specific twist: "because owners may be willing to tolerate losses, **the floor might actually be a level of profit less than zero**."
- **Debt is not a moral category.** (Szymanski notes the German *Schuld* means both debt and guilt.) All productive activity runs on credit; the question is only whether repayment is serviceable from operations. Deloitte, quoted approvingly: "If debt is sustainable and the repayment schedule is manageable within the cash generated from the club's operations, it can be used to facilitate and fund a growth strategy."
- **"Football club debt" is not one thing.** UEFA's own benchmarking report notes usage ranges "from the very broad, totaling all liabilities that a club has, to the narrow definition of debt financing either including or excluding **interest free owner loans**." **Always ask which definition a headline number uses** — soft owner loans are the single biggest source of apparent "debt" at sugar-daddy clubs.
- **Modigliani–Miller, applied** (Box 5.2): in the idealised case, the debt/equity mix does not change the value of a firm — it only changes how risk is shared. The real-world exceptions are what matter in football: **tax treatment favours debt**, and **insolvency has real transaction costs** that do destroy value.
- **Where the Premier League's outside capital actually came from** (Deloitte, first 20 years): ~$280m from stock-market flotations in the 1990s (most investors saw no return); ~$480m from media companies chasing broadcast access after Sky's blocked bid for United (also no obvious return); and **~$4bn from new owners in the following decade, ~$2.8bn of it from Abramovich and Mansour alone.** Real Premier League debt, once soft owner loans are stripped out, was only about **40% of the league's annual income.**

### 6 — Ownership
- **Core idea**: you cannot describe what clubs do without asking what they are *for*. "Talking about what soccer clubs do without discussing what they are for is a little bit like *Hamlet* without the prince." A club "cannot be said just to be the creature of its investors."
- **Clubs are geographically immobile in a way no other business is.** Service businesses replicate (McDonald's); clubs cannot. "**However much they try to reach a global audience, soccer clubs by their nature and history cannot multiply locations, and are forever tied to their city and region.**" Bavarians like BMWs, but BMW would fail selling only to Bavarians.
- **The identity function is the product.** Barcelona's *més que un club*; Athletic Bilbao's Basque-only recruitment; Standard Liège and the Walloons; Bayern and Bavaria. And the derby as "the struggle for control of the city — a phenomenon that can be traced back to Roman chariot racing." Szymanski's line: calling clubs "entertainment businesses" is "almost as profane as saying that the Catholic Church is in the counseling business."
- **But loyalty is not absolute**, and he says so against his own romanticism: attendance reliably collapses on relegation, which "absolute loyalty would require the fans to keep attending regardless." Some fans quietly start attending elsewhere — "the soccer equivalent of an extra-marital affair: it happens all the time but is seldom talked about."
- **The ownership shift**: English clubs adopted limited-liability structures only to fund stadium construction without directors bearing personal liability. Birley's judgement, quoted: "**Shareholding proved a singularly inappropriate method of determining control of League soccer.**" In 1984/85 most top clubs had roughly a thousand dispersed shareholders; by 2014/15 ownership was concentrated in a few hands — the same story across Europe.

### 7 — Strategy (the most directly usable chapter)
- **The wage curve — the numbers to actually carry.** From 21 seasons of Premier League accounts, expressed as a multiple of the **league-average wage bill**:
  | To finish… | you need to spend roughly |
  |---|---|
  | 1st | **over 4× the average** |
  | 2nd | **2.5× the average** |
  | 3rd | **just under 2×** |
  | 4th (Champions League) | **~1.5×** |
  | 7th | **exactly the average** |
  | relegation | **below 0.5×** |
- **The scale-invariance point that makes the curve usable across eras**: "How much you spend on the team doesn't matter in an absolute sense. **What matters is that you spent more than your rivals.**" United won the title in 1994 on £11m of wages and in 2013 on £183m — **both times just over twice the league average**, while the average rose from £5.3m to £89.4m.
- **These are averages, not certainties.** The most anyone spent in the period was just under 3× average (Chelsea, 2005 title); Arsenal spent nearly 2× and finished 4th in 2006; Blackburn spent 15% above average in 1999 **and were relegated**. "You can beat the averages by luck — which often happens — or you can try to beat the averages by strategy, which is much more difficult."
- **The revenue curve, and where the profit is.** For the bottom half of the league, wages are a fairly constant **60–70% of revenue**. But at the top, "the implied wage expenditure is actually **higher than the expected revenue**" — you must spend more than you earn to win. Chelsea and City did exactly that; United and Arsenal reported profits and had their profitability eroded by the others driving up prices. And the squeeze runs both ways: "**a mid-table position does not generate a profit on average**," because the clubs below spend almost as much as mid-table and relegation is close, so "the smaller teams have to spend heavily just to have a chance of hanging onto a Premier League place."
- **The historical contrast that dates the whole model.** Wage spending explains **56% of the variation in league position in the Premier League era, but only 13% in 1960–1980.** Back then the wage curve was very steep at the top and the revenue curve almost flat — because the player market barely functioned (maximum wage until 1961, no movement without permission until 1963, no foreign players until 1978, clubs colluding on pay). **Between 1960 and 1980, all but three champions spent less than 66% above average — and three spent *below* average (Burnley 1960, Ipswich 1962, Everton 1970). Since the Premier League began, all but three champions have spent more than 66% above average.**
- **The strategic conclusion**: "a club is fenced in on every side. Markets determine how much success it can achieve and how much revenue it can generate, history determines where the club is today, and **the future is determined by the breadth of the owner's ego, and the depth of his pockets**." Climbing requires someone to build a financial bridge with no expectation of repayment — "and, of course, luck always plays a role, so there is every possibility that the strategy fails and the club is left with a top-end wage bill and a middling ranking."
- **The manager, and the brand** (sub-sections): the manager "tends to be seen as a kind of Messiah… who can turn straw into gold — at least when things go well," where originally the board picked the team and the coach kept players fit. Brand value is what lets a dominant club sustain investment and convert success into revenue faster than a typical club — which is why dominance compounds.

### 8 — Insolvency
- **The legal frame, briefly**: individuals go *bankrupt*; companies become *insolvent*. Since the US Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978 (Chapter XI) and its imitators — England's **administration** (1985–86), Spain's *Ley Concursal* (2003), Italy (2005), Germany (2012) — the balance of power has swung **from creditors to debtors**. The cost: "Managers and shareholders know that they will profit if things go well, while **the creditors will bear the costs when things go wrong**. The 'too big to fail' problem… is not just relevant to the banking industry."
- **The finding that overturns the standard story.** The received narrative is Leeds United: overreach, chase the Champions League, crash. Szymanski's data on **48 English clubs entering insolvency for the first time between 1982 and 2010** says otherwise:
  - Their average league rank **twenty-two years before** insolvency was about **55** — mid-table in the third tier. "**It is not the big clubs that are succumbing to insolvency.**"
  - There was a modest rise (about eight places) between twenty and seven years out.
  - Then, in **the six years before insolvency, an average collapse of sixteen league places** — enough to guarantee relegation.
  > "**Clubs that become insolvent do not have a recent history of rising up the league but a recent history of falling down the league.**"
- **The financial mechanics of the fall**: attendance is 9,000–11,000 five years out and then **drops 27%**; revenue falls from ~80% of the league average to ~50%, lagging attendance by a year (season tickets are bought after a bad season, not before). The **wage-to-revenue ratio rises from ~65% to ~90%** — which by itself defines insolvency, since nothing is left for anything else.
- **And the directors were usually trying.** Wage spending relative to the league fell from above 70% to below 40% in the run-up. "**Falling revenues outpaced the fall in wages.**" It could hardly have been avoided: players have contracts, and "no player (or his agent) will willingly sign a contract that says he won't get paid if things go wrong at the club."
- **The final diagnosis, which is a general lesson about variance**: "By the law of large numbers you will have as much good luck as bad luck — eventually. But **in the short term it is perfectly possible to suffer from a long sequence of bad luck. And that, it appears, can lead even the unambitious to insolvency.**"

### 9 — America
The comparison chapter: closed leagues, salary caps, drafts and franchise relocation, versus Europe's open pyramid. The key asymmetry is that **closed leagues make owners rich and make redistribution easy**, because there is no entry threat — which is exactly why they are attractive to owners and unacceptable to European fans.

### 10 — Regulation
- **The paradox stated**: "Persistent dominance by a small elite, chronic financial crises, and widespread insolvency — any industry characterized in this way might be thought to be failing rather badly." Yet EU-28 GDP was still **1% below its 2007 level in 2013 while European top-division club revenues grew 28%** over 2007–2012.
- **What FFP actually requires**: a licence from the national association, with (a) **no overdue payables**, and (b) **break-even** — "relevant" income against "relevant" expenditure, measured over three years with a €5m acceptable deviation. Income counts tickets, merchandising, broadcasting and sponsorship; expenditure counts wages and transfers. Note that these definitions **do not coincide with accounting definitions**: "a club could in theory declare an accounting profit while failing to meet break-even, or declare an accounting loss but meet break even."
- **The etymological critique**: UEFA's stated objectives are about *efficiency*, not fairness. "There need be nothing fair about 'improving the economic and financial capability of clubs'… 'discipline and rationality' can be achieved without any appeal to fairness." Even the creditor-protection clause isn't self-evidently fair, since "creditors may have been guilty of imposing unreasonable conditions in the first place."
- **The prediction, made in 2015 and worth checking against what happened**: FFP "will only enhance the dominance of the big clubs without addressing the financial problems of the small teams." And the dilemma: "**If the regulations are fully enforced then they will harm the clubs they regulate. If the regulations are not enforced then the regulator's credibility will be undermined.**"

### 11 — Conclusion
- **Why the growth happened without anyone planning it**: rising income, more leisure, globalisation, broadcast technology — plus a speculative but interesting point about athletes as public figures. "The quality of their *athletic* performance is beyond doubt, unlike, say, the performance of bankers, CEOs, or politicians. They are genuine competitors — we see them compete; there is rarely any dispute about what they did." Football had no rival for global sporting attention, "so once the world started to spend money soccer just absorbed the funds that were thrown at it."
- **What he would and wouldn't do.** Would: **enforce sporting penalties for unpaid debts** (unpopular precisely because it falls on small clubs), and support **supporter trusts** in partnership with owners. Wouldn't: closed leagues, government regulation of an entertainment business, or FFP as constituted.
- **Why he defends the sugar daddies**: "I value competition precisely because of **the discipline it places on the owners**. I fear that regulatory systems are likely to relax competition and enable owners to take money out of the game… **I support the freedom of capitalists to lose a fortune investing in a soccer club.**"
- **The real risk he names**: not apocalypse but **the quiet death of promotion and relegation** — "it might never be formally abolished, but there may be ways in which smaller clubs are effectively barred from the highest level." He also makes the Super League's economic case honestly: in ten seasons, the eight clubs that won the Champions League played each other only **97 times** (17 of those domestic duplicates) — once or twice a season each, with some pairings unplayed for a decade.
- **The closing sentence of the argument**: "Promotion and relegation is the cause of dominance and distress, and all the stress and anxiety that is borne by soccer fans across the world. **But it is also the source of never-ending hope.**"

## Worked Example
**Reading a "club in crisis" story with the wage curve and the insolvency profile.**

A mid-table Premier League club has just been relegated, is reported to be carrying £90m of "debt," has sacked its manager, and the coverage says it overreached chasing Europe. Work through it in Szymanski's order:

1. **Where was it on the wage curve?** If its wage bill was near the league average, it was structurally a **7th-place club** — and 7th is not a springboard, it is the equilibrium. If it was at 1.5×, it was buying a Champions League place and failed; if it was at 0.6×, relegation was the modal outcome, not a shock. **The bill sets the expectation; only the residual is a decision.**
2. **Then check the revenue side.** In the bottom half, wages should be 60–70% of revenue. If they are at 90%, the club is already insolvent in substance, whatever the accounts say — that is the exact ratio Szymanski found at the point of legal insolvency.
3. **Then check the trajectory, not the ambition.** His 48-club dataset says insolvent clubs are **third-tier-average clubs that have fallen sixteen places in six years**, with attendance down 27% and revenue down from 80% to 50% of the league average. The Leeds story — overreach from the top — is the *memorable* case, not the *typical* one. **Ask whether this club was climbing or falling.**
4. **Then decompose the "debt."** How much is soft owner loans? Once those were stripped out, real Premier League debt was only ~40% of annual income. A number that includes interest-free shareholder loans is a number about ownership structure, not distress.
5. **Then price relegation properly.** The top-tier/second-tier revenue gap is roughly **seven-to-one**, up from about 1.5:1 in the 1960s. That is why relegation now breaks clubs that would have survived it fifty years ago — and it is a fact about the *structure*, not about this board's competence.
6. **Only now discuss the manager.** Wages and squad value already explain most of the position; the sacking is where the club's own narrative starts, not where the causation does.

**The transferable lesson**: the finances are the *constraint set*, not the *explanation*. Establish what the money made likely, and then — and only then — argue about the residual.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- **Establish what the relative wage bill predicted before judging any decision.** Absolute spending is meaningless; the multiple of the league average is everything. Roughly: 4× to win, 2.5× for second, 1.5× for the top four, 1× for seventh, below 0.5× for relegation.
- **Respect the scale of the claim.** The book reports wage relationships of ~5–10% for a single match, 56% for season league position in its Premier League sample, and ~90% over the long run. These are source-era estimates, not portable constants. Resources may constrain lineup options in one game, but cannot establish the cause of a particular incident.
- **Don't transplant the wage curve across eras or leagues without checking.** It explained only **13%** of variation in England in 1960–80, when the player market was closed. The curve is a property of an *efficient labour market*, not of football.
- **Expect zero profit at every league position, and treat sustained profit as a signal.** In an open league, profit is competed away. A consistently profitable club is either exceptional (United), under-investing (Arsenal), or protected by something worth identifying.
- **A mid-table position does not generate a profit.** There is no safe, cheap plateau in a modern top division — the clubs below spend nearly as much and relegation is one bad run away.
- **Ask which definition of "debt" a headline uses**, and specifically whether interest-free owner loans are included. Also ask whether the reported loss is cash or accounting.
- **Judge distress by trajectory, not ambition.** Insolvent clubs are falling clubs — sixteen places in six years, attendance −27%, revenue from 80% to 50% of average, wages from 65% to 90% of revenue. Overreach is the famous story; decline is the usual one.
- **Don't confuse individual crisis with systemic crisis.** "You don't treat the common cold in the same way that you treat a heart attack." Clubs almost never die.
- **Treat dominance as structural, not moral.** It is the same in Germany and the Faroe Islands, and it has not measurably intensified in fifty years. If you claim money caused it, produce the earlier balanced era.
- **Price relegation as a seven-to-one revenue cliff**, and remember that within-division inequality has barely moved in thirty years. The inequality that grew is *between* divisions.
- **Remember the two-sided nature of the wage-position correlation.** Causation is not proven, reverse causation is possible, and **paying more does not make a given player better** — it buys access to better players.
- **In a single match, expect the expensive team to fail to win half the time.** The advantage only becomes near-certain over a season. Say "over 38 games" out loud when you invoke it.
- **Before calling a stadium project reckless, ask about the honeymoon and the emptiness risk.** New grounds lift attendance 30%+ for years — unless the capacity is so far above demand that the emptiness itself deters people (South Africa 2010).
- **When evaluating a financial regulation, ask who it protects.** Szymanski's test: does it restrain spending, or does it restrain *entry*? FFP's break-even rule constrains challengers more than incumbents.
- **Remember that clubs are geographically immobile identity institutions**, which is why fan-facing decisions are not interchangeable with ordinary business decisions — and also why fan loyalty is real but not unconditional (attendance falls on relegation).
- **Spending precedes revenue in every industry.** A club borrowing to buy a squad is doing what a farmer does buying seed. The question is serviceability, not virtue.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Promotion and relegation causes both dominance and distress.** Open entry drives expected profit to zero at every position, so only clubs that can reinvest winnings stay on top and everyone else lives near the edge.
2. **Relative wage bill is the single best predictor of league position** — a wages-only model correlates ~0.83 with bookmakers' odds — and the effect works through the law of large numbers, not through any single match.
3. **The wage curve is the practical instrument**: 4× average to win, 1.5× for the top four, 1× for seventh, under 0.5× for relegation — and it is scale-invariant across decades.
4. **Winning and profit are incompatible at the top.** Above a certain league position the required wage bill exceeds expected revenue, which is why serious ambition requires an owner willing to lose money.
5. **Insolvent clubs are falling clubs, not overreaching ones** — typically third-tier-average sides that dropped sixteen places in six years while wages rose to 90% of shrinking revenue.
6. **Debt is neither immoral nor informative on its own.** Strip out owner loans, check serviceability against operating cash, and distinguish accounting losses from cash outflows.
7. **The system is healthy even when its clubs are not**, and the real threat is not insolvency but the quiet erosion of promotion and relegation — "the source of never-ending hope."
