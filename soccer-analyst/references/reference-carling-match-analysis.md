# Handbook of Soccer Match Analysis: A Systematic Approach to Improving Performance — Christopher Carling, A. Mark Williams & Thomas Reilly
**Format**: supplied Markdown | **Edition**: 2005; supplied e-library edition 2007 | **Sections**: 9 | **Depth**: study
**Use for**: turning a match impression into reliable observations, useful feedback and a testable training adjustment.

## Mental Model (read first)
Match analysis is part of the coaching process: decide what needs improving, collect information that can answer that question, evaluate it, and use it in practice. The quality of a database depends on its definitions and observers; more entries do not repair an incomplete view of play. Notational analysis records actions and their outcomes, while motion analysis records movement and work rate. Neither interpretation nor a statistical total substitutes for seeing the relevant football context.

**Source boundary:** Technology descriptions, tournament frequencies and physical benchmarks belong to this edition. The procedures below are original distillations. The explicit record schema, comparison safeguards and follow-up checks are advisor applications, not an author-named universal protocol. ## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapters 1–2 — From a coaching question to a notation system
**Question:** What exactly keeps going wrong, and which observations could distinguish the explanations? **Required evidence:** the coach's intended action, suitable match footage or direct observation, and enough context to identify players and phases.

The four stages of manual notation are deciding what information is needed and why, designing the system, checking accuracy, and collating/presenting findings. Start with a small system, trial it, obtain feedback from its user, then add complexity. A question about poor crossing can concern access to the flank, delivery, or the receiving forwards; a count of crosses alone cannot distinguish these.

- **Player:** identify the actor and role; decide whether to observe the team, a unit or an individual. Log substitutions and role changes so one shirt number is not treated as one constant role.
- **Action:** define both the event and its outcome before coding. A completed pass, a pass allowing the receiver to continue in stride, and a dangerous pass are different attributes. Use categories that separate blocked, saved, off-target and scoring shots without changing definitions after seeing results.
- **Position:** define pitch zones or a coordinate convention, attacking direction and origin. Location should distinguish relevant tactical alternatives rather than create unnecessary coding work.
- **Time and sequence:** preserve order and match time; optional time bands can reveal changes. A late concession may follow loss of possession, lost challenges or altered roles. Detailed sequences usually need replay, whereas a few live counts can support half-time decisions.

**Advisor record schema:** match/clip identifier; timestamp and phase; player/role; action and outcome; start/end zone; preceding trigger and next action; score/manpower state; visible support/opposition; missing or uncertain view. Keep a separate observation field and interpretation field. This schema operationalizes the book's components; it is not a copied form.

**Do not omit silently:** unavailable off-ball positioning, a blocked camera view or an uncertain actor belongs in the record as missing information. If only deliveries are coded, do not attribute failure to forwards' runs. Define the opportunity denominator: attempts per relevant possession or delivery success per cross answers a different question from raw totals.

### Chapters 2 and 4 — Accuracy, reliability and observer preparation
**Question:** Would another trained observer describe the same events the same way? Pilot definitions on recorded sequences, compare independent coding, discuss disagreements, revise ambiguous categories, and recode affected material. One observer can repeat a video segment later; compare live coding with replay when turnaround forces live work. Recheck after changing observer, system or definition, and periodically for definition drift.

The book discusses comparing totals and correlations as practical checks. **Advisor safeguard:** agreement on totals can conceal disagreement about individual events, and correlation can coexist with systematic overcounting. Inspect timestamped event disagreements and location/outcome categories as well. Report the check actually performed; do not invent an accepted reliability threshold or call a system valid merely because it is repeatable.

Train observers on both replay and the intended live conditions. Confirm line-ups, identifying details, timing and equipment beforehand. Choose the level of detail against the speed of feedback needed. A complex form that misses actions under time pressure is less useful than a smaller reliable one.

### Chapter 3 — Video, computerized analysis and databases
**Question:** Which evidence can the available capture method actually provide? Link coded events to replayable clips. A database supports consistent retrieval across matches; video permits qualitative review; player tracking can reveal positions missing from a broadcast view. Check field of view, synchronization, player identity and storage/retrieval before trusting the output.

The catalogue of cameras, editing systems, tracking products and costs is historical. Retain the functional distinctions, not procurement advice. A reconstruction or software label remains a representation with measurement error. No tracking available means no invented full-pitch movement paths.

### Chapter 5 — Evaluation and presentation
Separate technical, tactical, physical and behavioural questions, and in-possession from out-of-possession performance. Interpret counts against opportunities, roles, opposition and game circumstances. A safe passer's 80% completion need not be better than a progressive passer's 50%; less distance may reflect a holding assignment. Quantity is not quality.

Look for stability across relevant matches, but do not impose a fixed universal match count: shots depend on supply, opponent and shot location, so a player's profile may remain variable. Check venue, conditions, quality, competition stakes, referee incidents and chance. Treat psychological explanations as hypotheses requiring evidence, not as diagnoses from a missed shot.

Communicate the few points players can act on. Connect each point to a short sequence; show it at normal speed before selective replay or freeze-frame. Include successful and unsuccessful examples, invite players' accounts, and match detail to audience and situation. The book's team-talk scenario combines an opponent's system, strengths and weaknesses with clips rather than overwhelming players with tables.

### Chapter 6 — Motion analysis and training demands
**Question:** Does the observed movement pattern indicate a role demand or a physical limitation? Record durations or distances by defined intensity/activity categories, alongside positional role, style and match conditions. Exercise-to-rest patterns and repeated high-intensity work carry information that total distance loses. Acceleration, deceleration and direction changes matter to interpretation even when total distance looks similar.

A lower work rate alone cannot establish fatigue, poor effort or inadequate fitness. Combine movement evidence with role and, when appropriate, qualified physical assessment. Historical distance ranges, speed categories and physiological advice are not present-day individualized prescriptions. Keep conditioning decisions within appropriate expertise.

### Chapter 7 — Set plays, open play and consecutive forward movement
Study set plays as sequences: award location, preparation, delivery, attacking and defending organization, first contact, subsequent action and outcome. For open play, retain regain location, passes, possession duration, actions preceding penetration and shot location. **Consecutive forward movement** directs attention to the connected progression towards goal, rather than a single isolated pass.

The chapter's World Cup and domestic goal distributions describe its samples. A high share of goals following short moves does not establish that any team should minimize passes: goal-only counts omit how often each kind of move was attempted and failed. To recommend a change, add comparable non-scoring possessions and consider what possession protects as well as what it creates. This denominator safeguard is an advisor synthesis, not a claim that the book estimates a causal effect.

### Chapter 8 — From analysis to drills
Distinguish strategy (overall plan), playing system (organization), tactics (means used in situations) and technique (execution). In the book's crossing, shooting and goalkeeping scenarios, an initial concern becomes observations, evaluation, feedback and a practice design.

Translate a supported issue into a specific cue or responsibility, then a representative exercise with relevant opposition and constraints. Make the success criterion observable. Check the same feature in later matches and note changed context; improvement after training is encouraging but does not by itself isolate a causal effect. Do not generalize one historical crossing exercise into a modern build-up or pressing curriculum.

### Chapter 9 — Future analysis and coding logic
The useful design principle is to let already-recorded information reduce repeated input: a sequence of identified players, positions and outcomes can imply connected actions. However, ambiguous control, deflections or identity still require a check. The chapter's forecasts about voice recognition, GPS, expert systems and performance-related pay are source-era possibilities, not evidence of current system capability or fair player valuation.

## Worked Example — Diagnose missing width before blaming the finishers
Reconstruction of Chapter 8's **Crossing Analysis Scenario**, not a newly observed match. A 4–4–2 side intends to release its full-backs but the coach suspects insufficient width. The scenario reports 59% possession, five clear chances and only two full-back crosses; zone evidence shows the full-backs staying deep and attacks concentrated centrally. Across five matches, only two of ten crosses find an attacker.

The analysts combine counts with positional reconstruction and clips. Players then supply a missing explanation: the forwards have been moving wide themselves and are absent from the box. The proposed adjustment coordinates full-back overlaps with forwards' timed arrivals, and training rehearses those relationships. The book sets crossing targets for this example; they are not universal standards.

**What the example demonstrates:** possession share cannot diagnose the problem, delivery and receiving movement are coupled, and feedback becomes a concrete practice task. **Advisor follow-up:** code comparable wide-entry opportunities and box occupation after the change, including unsuccessful sequences. Do not claim the drill worked without that later evidence.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When an impression contains several possible causes, choose observations that distinguish them before counting everything available.
- When definitions differ, fix and recode before comparing players or matches.
- When coding changes between live and replay, report the discrepancy and use the appropriate confidence for the decision.
- When a total looks bad, check opportunity, role and surrounding movement before assigning fault.
- When only goals or highlights were sampled, collect comparable ordinary and unsuccessful sequences before inferring a recurring mechanism.
- When feedback cannot specify an action players can try, revisit the analytical question.
- When a camera cannot show the claimed off-ball cause, keep that cause unverified.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with the coaching decision, then define the evidence.
2. Player, action, position and time/sequence make observations inspectable.
3. Reliability, contextual interpretation and concise feedback connect analysis to useful practice.
4. Recheck the proposed adjustment; do not confuse a convincing explanation with a demonstrated improvement.
